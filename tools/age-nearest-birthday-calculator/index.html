How it works

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter the date of birth — the full birth date from the birth record.
  2. Set the reference date — the policy issue date, the valuation date, or today.
  3. Calculate — the engine counts the exact days to the last birthday and to the next birthday.
  4. Read the nearest birthday — the closer anniversary decides the age; the result also shows both day counts and the completed chronological age.

Method

Method: nearer birthday wins, ties round up

The engine takes the birth date and the reference date, both as UTC calendar dates. It finds the two surrounding birthday anniversaries: the last birthday on or before the reference date, and the next birthday after it. It counts the exact calendar days in both directions, using real Gregorian month lengths and leap days. One count runs from the last birthday to the reference date; the other runs from the reference date to the next birthday. The nearer anniversary decides the age. If the last birthday is closer, the Age Nearest Birthday equals the age at last birthday. If the next birthday is closer, it equals the age at next birthday.

When both distances are exactly equal — possible only in a 366-day interval, on day 183 — the engine rounds up to the age at next birthday. That is the actuarial round-half-up rule. A 29 February birthday is clamped to 28 February in non-leap years when the surrounding anniversaries are built, so the day counts stay continuous. No averaging by 365.25 is used, and no 30-day month either. The midpoint of a 365-day year is 182.5 days, so day 182 still belongs to the last birthday and day 183 belongs to the next.

Reading your result

Reading your result

The result panel reports five figures. Each one answers a different question:

  • Age Nearest Birthday — the headline rating age, in whole years, used to look up premium tables.
  • Nearest birthday — the calendar date of the anniversary that decided the age.
  • Closer milestone — whether the last or the next birthday governed, with the exact day gap.
  • Age at last birthday — the completed whole-year age, for checking which tier you are in under the completed-age convention.
  • Completed chronological age — years, months and days lived, for comparison with the rounded rating age.

Limits

What this tool does not do

  • The result is a rating convention, not a legal age. Voting, licensing and benefits use completed age, not the nearest birthday.
  • The tie rule (round up) follows the actuarial convention built into this engine. An insurer that uses a different tie rule can return a different age on the exact midpoint day.
  • A 29 February birthday is clamped to 28 February in non-leap years here. Some jurisdictions use 1 March instead, which shifts the day counts by one.
  • The calculator cannot tell you which convention your policy uses. Check the policy wording for “age nearest birthday”, “age at last birthday” or “age at next birthday” before quoting.
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Authoritative answers on nearest birthday reckoning, day counts, and midpoint rules.

What does an Age Nearest Birthday Calculator do?

It calculates which annual birthday anniversary—the previous one or the upcoming one—is closer to a selected reference date, and determines your age based on that nearest birthday.

How is the nearest birthday determined?

The calculator counts the exact calendar days from your most recent birthday to the reference date, and from the reference date to your upcoming birthday. Whichever birthday has the smaller day distance is your nearest birthday.

What is the difference between nearest birthday and last birthday?

Age at last birthday counts only completed full years lived. Age nearest birthday rounds to the upcoming birthday once you pass the half-year midpoint (about 183 days after your last birthday).

What happens when the reference date is exactly halfway between birthdays?

When a reference date falls on an exact midpoint (such as 183 days in a 366-day leap year interval), standard actuarial and insurance convention applies the round-half-up rule, rounding up to the next birthday.

How are February 29 birthdays handled?

For a 29 February birthday, this calculator clamps the anniversary to 28 February in non-leap years when it builds the surrounding birthdays, so the day counts stay continuous and the midpoint rule still applies.
