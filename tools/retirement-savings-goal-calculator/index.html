1. What Is a Retirement Savings Goal Calculator?

A retirement savings goal calculator answers the reverse of the usual retirement question. Instead of “what will my savings grow to?”, it answers “what must I save each month to reach the amount I want?” You state the target, the time available, what you have already saved, and the return you expect — and the calculator solves for the monthly contribution that closes the gap.

That framing matters because the target is usually the fixed part of a retirement plan: a lifestyle costs what it costs. The monthly saving is the variable you control, and it changes sharply with time — starting ten years earlier can cut the required monthly amount by more than half, because growth does more of the work.

Everything runs in your browser — your ages, savings and target are never sent anywhere.

2. How to Use This Calculator

There are five inputs. Current age and retirement age set the time available, in months. Current savings is what you have already put aside for retirement (enter 0 if you are starting now). Target at retirement is the amount you want to have on the day you retire. Expected annual return is the rate your investments are assumed to earn, as a percentage per year.

Select Calculate Monthly Saving and the results panel shows the headline figure — the monthly contribution required — alongside the months to retirement, the total you will contribute, the growth that makes up the rest, and the target. The milestone table fills in with your projected balance every 5 years. Reset clears all inputs; Copy result puts a one-line summary on your clipboard.

3. How the Monthly Figure Is Calculated

The calculation uses monthly compounding. Let r be the monthly return (annual return ÷ 12), n the number of months to retirement, PV your current savings, and FV your target. Your current savings grow to PV × (1 + r)n. The remaining gap must be filled by monthly contributions, and a monthly contribution P grows to P × ((1 + r)n − 1) ÷ r. Solving for P gives the required monthly saving: P = (FV − PV × (1 + r)n) × r ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1).

If the expected return is zero, the answer is simply the gap divided by the months: (FV − PV) ÷ n. If your current savings alone grow to the target or beyond, the required monthly saving is zero or negative — the calculator reports that you are already on track and shows the projected value of your current savings instead of a meaningless negative saving.

The milestone projection applies the same math at each 5-year point: the grown value of current savings plus the grown value of the monthly contributions made so far.

4. Worked Examples

Example 1 — starting from zero. Age 30, retiring at 60 (360 months), no current savings, target ₹2 crore, expected return 10% per year. The required saving is ₹8,848 per month. Over 30 years you contribute ₹31,85,153 and growth supplies the remaining ₹1,68,14,847 — more than five times what you put in.

Example 2 — a head start. Age 35, retiring at 60 (300 months), current savings ₹5 lakh, target ₹1 crore, expected return 8% per year. The required saving is ₹6,656 per month — lower than Example 1 despite the smaller target, because the existing ₹5 lakh does 25 years of compounding work.

Example 3 — no return at all. Age 40, retiring at 60 (240 months), no current savings, target ₹12 lakh, expected return 0%. The required saving is simply ₹12,00,000 ÷ 240 = ₹5,000 per month, which is a useful check that the formula behaves when growth is absent.

5. Limits & Notes

This is a projection, not a promise. It assumes a constant annual return, monthly contributions at each month-end, and no taxes, fees, inflation or salary growth. Real returns vary year to year, and a constant-return projection will differ from any actual outcome — that is why the return input is worth stress-testing: run the same target at a lower return and see how much the monthly figure rises.

The target is a nominal figure: ₹2 crore in 30 years buys less than ₹2 crore today. If you want a target in today’s purchasing power, enter the inflation-adjusted amount instead — roughly, multiply today’s target by (1 + inflation)years. The calculator deliberately does not add an inflation assumption of its own, because that would be an invented input dressed up as a result.

The result is also not financial advice. Pension rules, tax treatment and employer contributions all change the picture for an individual, and a qualified adviser should review any retirement plan before it is relied on.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

What does this calculator work out?

The monthly contribution you need to make, from now until your retirement age, to reach a target amount at retirement, given your current savings and an expected annual return.

What formula does it use?

Monthly compounding. With monthly return r, months n, current savings PV and target FV, the required monthly saving is (FV − PV × (1 + r)^n) × r ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1). At zero return it is simply the gap divided by the months.

What if my current savings already reach the target?

The calculator says you are already on track and shows the projected value of your current savings at retirement, instead of a negative monthly saving.

What return should I assume?

Use a conservative long-run assumption for the investments you actually hold, and test a lower one too. The return is an assumption you supply, not a prediction the calculator makes, and the monthly figure moves sharply with it.

Does the result include inflation?

No. The target is a nominal amount at retirement. If you want the target in today’s purchasing power, enter the inflation-adjusted figure: today’s target × (1 + inflation)^years.

Are taxes and fees included?

No. Taxes, fund charges and adviser fees are not deducted, so the required saving is a pre-tax, pre-fee figure. Your actual required saving may be higher once those are counted.

Are contributions monthly or yearly?

Monthly, assumed paid at each month-end and compounded monthly. If you save yearly, divide the monthly figure by 12 only as a rough guide, because yearly contributions compound differently.

Can I change my retirement age to see the effect?

Yes. Retiring later adds months of contributions and compounding, which lowers the monthly figure; retiring earlier raises it. Run both ages to see the trade-off.

Why does starting early matter so much?

Because growth compounds. In the worked example, a 30-year-old saving ₹8,848 a month contributes ₹31,85,153 in total and growth supplies ₹1,68,14,847 — more than five times the contributions. Starting later shifts that balance toward your own money.

Is the result guaranteed?

No. It is a projection under a constant-return assumption. Real returns vary, and the outcome will differ. Treat the figure as a planning guide, not a guarantee, and review it as your circumstances change.

Is this calculator free, and are my figures private?

Yes, it is free, and it runs entirely in your browser. Your ages, savings and target are never sent to a server or stored anywhere.

8. Methodology & Data Integrity

The math is the standard future-value-of-an-annuity formula with monthly compounding, solved for the payment. No return, inflation or fee assumption is built in: every assumption is an input you supply, and the page states that the result is a projection, not a prediction or advice.

Worked examples on this page were produced by the calculator’s own engine: age 30 to 60, ₹0 to ₹2 crore at 10% = ₹8,848 per month (₹31,85,153 contributed, ₹1,68,14,847 growth); age 35 to 60, ₹5 lakh to ₹1 crore at 8% = ₹6,656 per month; zero-return case ₹12 lakh over 240 months = ₹5,000 per month. Monthly figures are rounded to the nearest rupee.

This tool does not give financial advice and does not account for tax, fees, inflation, salary growth or pension rules. A retirement plan built on it should be reviewed by a qualified adviser.
