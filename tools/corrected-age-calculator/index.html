How it works

Get your Corrected Age result in four clear steps

Enter the date of birth, the date to calculate on and the gestational age at birth in weeks, then select Calculate. The explanation is broken into short actions so you can understand the workflow without reading a wall of text.

1

Date of birth

Enter the baby’s actual date of birth. Chronological age is counted from this date first, before any prematurity correction is applied.

2

Calculate on

Enter the date you want the corrected age for: today, or another date such as an appointment or a milestone check.

3

Gestational age at birth (weeks)

Enter the gestational age at birth in weeks; values from 20 to 40 are accepted. The earlier the birth, the larger the correction, and at 40 weeks the correction is zero, so corrected age equals chronological age.

4

Select Calculate and read the corrected age

The tool subtracts the prematurity adjustment — (40 minus the gestational weeks) multiplied by 7 days — from the chronological age: born 1 June 2026 at 32 weeks, a baby’s corrected age on 20 October 2026 is 12 weeks, 1 day. If the adjustment is larger than the baby’s age, the result is shown as 0, never negative.

What you get

Age adjusted for the weeks a baby arrived early

Enter the date of birth, the date to calculate on, and the gestational age at birth in weeks. The calculator works out the chronological age in days, subtracts the prematurity adjustment — (40 − gestational weeks) × 7 days — and reports the corrected age in weeks and days.

◌Method

The standard correction

Corrected age = chronological age − weeks premature, where weeks premature is counted against a 40-week full term. A baby born at 32 weeks arrived 8 weeks early, so 56 days are subtracted from the chronological age. Gestational ages from 20 to 40 weeks are accepted; at 40 weeks the adjustment is zero and corrected age equals chronological age.

◎Example

Worked example: born 1 June 2026 at 32 weeks

On 20 October 2026 this baby’s chronological age is 141 days (20 weeks). Subtracting the 56-day adjustment gives a corrected age of 85 days — reported as 12 weeks, 1 day. Enter the same dates and 32 weeks and the calculator returns the same result.

⌁Limits

Very early babies and other limits

If the adjustment is larger than the baby’s chronological age, the corrected age is shown as 0 rather than a negative number: a baby born at 28 weeks has an 84-day adjustment but is only 42 days old at 6 weeks of age. Corrected age is a developmental yardstick, not a medical judgement; how long it is used for an individual child is a matter for their clinicians.

Common questions

Corrected Age Calculator FAQ

Answers about method, inputs, privacy, boundaries and responsible interpretation.

What is corrected age?

It is a preterm baby's chronological age reduced by the time they arrived early, so development can be compared against the date they were due rather than the date they were born. The correction is counted against a 40-week full term.

How is the correction calculated?

The calculator takes the chronological age in days and subtracts (40 minus the gestational weeks at birth) multiplied by 7. A baby born at 32 weeks arrived 8 weeks early, so 56 days are subtracted; in the page's example that turns a chronological age of 141 days into a corrected age of 85 days, reported as 12 weeks, 1 day.

What gestational ages can I enter?

Gestational ages from 20 to 40 weeks are accepted. At 40 weeks the adjustment is zero, so corrected age equals chronological age, and the closer the birth was to full term, the smaller the adjustment becomes.

Why does the calculator show a corrected age of 0 for my baby?

That happens when the prematurity adjustment is larger than the baby's chronological age so far: the result is shown as 0 rather than a negative number. A baby born at 28 weeks has an 84-day adjustment but is only 42 days old at 6 weeks of age, so the corrected age still reads 0 then.

How long should corrected age be used for my child?

Corrected age is a developmental yardstick, not a medical judgement, and this page does not set a stopping point. How long it is used for an individual child is a matter for their clinicians, who know the child's history, so raise the question with them rather than relying on a general rule.
