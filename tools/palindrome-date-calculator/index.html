1. What is a palindrome date?

A palindrome date is a date whose digits read the same forwards and backwards. Write the date as one digit string — day, month and year in some order, zero-padded, separators removed — and check whether that string matches its own reversal. If it does, the date is a palindrome date in that format. The word comes from the Greek palin (again) and dromos (running): the digits run back the way they came.

The classic example is 2 February 2020. In US order it is 02022020, in European order it is also 02022020, and in ISO order it is 20200202 — all three read the same backwards. That is why it was covered worldwide: it is one of very few dates that are palindromes in every major format at once. A more extreme case is 11 November 1111, which reads 11111111 in all three 8-digit formats — eight identical digits, the same whichever way you order them.

Two rules decide everything else on this page. First, zero-padding counts: the day and month are always written with two digits, so 2 March is 02 and 03, not 2 and 3 — the padding changes the digit string, and therefore the verdict. Second, the format is part of the question: “is this date a palindrome?” is meaningless until you say in which order the digits are written. This tool checks six orders — US, European and ISO, each in 8-digit and 6-digit form — and the content below explains why the answers differ.

2. Why the format changes the answer

Take 2 March 2030, the next US-format palindrome date. In US order (month, day, year) it is 03022030, which reversed is 03022030 — a palindrome. In ISO order (year, month, day) it is 20300302, which reversed is also 20300302 — a palindrome. But in European order (day, month, year) it is 02032030, which reversed is 03032020 — not a palindrome. One calendar day, three verdicts, because each format builds a different digit string from the same date.

The reverse happens too. 3 February 2030 is a palindrome in European order (03022030 — the same digit string as 2 March 2030 in US order, which is why the two dates are sometimes confused) but not in US or ISO order. And 2 February 2020 is the rare date that works everywhere: 02022020 in both US and European order, 20200202 in ISO order. When a news story calls a date “a palindrome date” without naming a format, it usually means the US 8-digit form — but the honest answer always names the format, which is why this tool makes you pick one.

The 6-digit forms add a second layer. 2 February 2020 is not a 6-digit palindrome in any order: US short form gives 020220, reversed 022020 — not a match. Short forms produce palindromes on different dates from long forms, and usually more often, because a 6-digit string has fewer constraints to satisfy. Switching formats on the same date is the quickest way to see this: enter any date, run it through all six formats, and watch the verdict flip.

3. How the check works

The check has three steps. First, the date is written as digits: the day and month are zero-padded to two digits, and the year is written with four digits for 8-digit formats or two digits for 6-digit formats. Second, the digits are joined in the format’s order with no separators — so 2 March 2030 in US 8-digit form becomes 03022030. Third, the string is compared with its own reversal: if every digit matches its mirror position, the date is a palindrome in that format.

The previous, next and next-10 results come from stepping one day at a time from the entered date, in the proleptic Gregorian calendar, and testing each day the same way. “Previous” is the nearest palindrome strictly before the entered date; “next” is the nearest strictly after it; the next-10 list continues the forward scan. If the entered date itself is a palindrome, it is shown as the verdict, and the previous/next results are the neighbours around it. The weekday shown for every result comes from the same day count, so it always agrees with the calendar.

The supported range is years 1 to 9999. Dates before the Gregorian reform of 1582 are labelled proleptically — that is, as if the Gregorian calendar had always existed — which is the standard convention for date arithmetic, but not how those dates were written at the time. The tool checks digit strings only: a palindrome date is a numerical curiosity, not a lucky day, and the tool makes no claim beyond the digits.

4. The famous palindrome dates

2 February 2020 is the most famous palindrome date of modern times, because it worked in every major format and fell on a Sunday that newsrooms could plan around. It was also the first “universal” palindrome date since 11 November 1111 — the all-ones date, 11111111 in every 8-digit order — though 11/11/1111 predates the Gregorian calendar by centuries and is a proleptic curiosity rather than a date anyone wrote at the time.

The most recent US-format palindrome date before today is 2 December 2021 (12022021), and the next one is 2 March 2030 (03022030) — the same digit string, incidentally, as 3 February 2030 in European order. The first US-format palindrome date of the current millennium was 2 October 2001 (10022001). Between 2001 and 2021 there were only five US-format palindrome dates in total (2 October 2001, 2 January 2010, 2 November 2011, 2 February 2020 and 2 December 2021), which is why each one felt like an event: they arrive years apart, not yearly.

22 February 2022 deserves a careful note, because it was widely celebrated as “22/2/22” — and it is a palindrome only in unpadded form. Written without padding, 22/2/22 gives the digit string 22222, which reads the same backwards. But in any zero-padded form it fails: US short form gives 022222, European short form gives 220222, and neither reverses to itself. The celebration was real, but it belonged to the unpadded form — the subject of section 6 — and this is the clearest proof that padding is part of the definition, not a detail.

5. Why some years have many and others none

Palindrome dates are not spread evenly across years, and the reason is pure reversal arithmetic. For a US 8-digit palindrome (month, day, year), the year’s digits must be the reverse of the month-and-day digits. In the 21st century the year starts with 20, so the reversed month-and-day must start with 02 — which means the day must be the 2nd of the month, and the month must be the reverse of the year’s last two digits. That is why all 12 US-format palindrome dates of this century fall on the 2nd of a month: 2 October 2001, 2 January 2010, 2 November 2011, 2 February 2020, 2 December 2021, 2 March 2030, 2 April 2040, 2 May 2050, 2 June 2060, 2 July 2070, 2 August 2080 and 2 September 2090 — one per qualifying year, and no year qualifies twice, because a year has only one reverse.

The European 8-digit form works the other way round: the day-and-month digits reversed must form the year. In this century the year starts with 20, so the reversed day-and-month must start with 02 — which means the month must be February (02), and the day’s reverse supplies the year’s last two digits. That is why all 29 European-format palindrome dates of this century fall in February, from 10 February 2001 through 29 February 2092, with the day running through the values whose reverses give valid years. The ISO 8-digit form behaves like the US form with the month and year swapped: its 12 palindrome dates of the century also all fall on the 2nd of a month.

This also explains the gaps. A year like 2022 cannot produce a US-format palindrome at all: its reverse, 2202, would need month 22, which does not exist. A year like 2030 produces exactly one (2 March). And no year produces two in the same format, because the reversal fixes the month and day uniquely. Palindrome dates are rare not by accident but by arithmetic: most years’ reverses are not valid dates.

6. Palindrome weeks: the unpadded exception

Everything so far assumed zero-padding. Drop the padding, and a completely different pattern appears: palindrome weeks. In unpadded month-day-year form, most years since 2011 have had 10 consecutive palindrome days — 2020 has none at all, and 2022 has only 9, because February has 28 days that year. The mechanism is simple: write the month as one digit, the day as two digits and the year as two digits, and the string becomes month + day + year-last-two — for July 2027, the days 20 to 29 July give 72027, 72127, 72227 and so on up to 72927, every one of which reads the same backwards. The month always equals the year’s last digit (July for 2027, August for 2028, September for 2029), which is why the week moves one month later each year.

July 2027 is a good example: the 10 consecutive days from 20 to 29 July, 72027 to 72927. The same count held in June 2026 (20 to 29 June, 62026 to 62926). These weeks are the reason short-form palindrome dates feel common in some years and absent in others: they exist only in the unpadded form, and in most years they arrive on a schedule fixed by the calendar itself — years ending in 0 have none at all.

The padding distinction is worth stating plainly, because it is the most common source of confusion. 20 July 2027 zero-padded is 07202027, which reversed is 72027070 — not a palindrome. Unpadded it is 72027 — a palindrome. Both statements are true; they answer different questions. This tool computes the zero-padded forms, which are the standard for date-format palindromes, and this section records the unpadded weeks separately so the two are never mixed up.

7. The rarest palindrome dates

The rarest palindrome date of this century is 29 February 2092, in European order: 29022092, reversed 29022092. It is the only 29 February palindrome of the century in any 8-digit format, because a leap-day palindrome needs the day-month 2902 to reverse into a valid year — 2092 is the only leap year in the century whose reverse works — and it is also the last European-format palindrome date of the century. A date that is simultaneously a leap day, a palindrome and the last of its kind for a hundred years is as rare as palindrome dates get.

The last US-format palindrome date of the century is 2 September 2090 (09022090), and the last US-format palindrome date of the whole millennium is 22 September 2290 (09222290) — the 36th and final US-format palindrome date between 2001 and 3000. After 2290 there are no more US-format palindrome dates until the next millennium’s pattern begins, because the reversal arithmetic runs out of valid months. The ISO format has its own long gaps: the published analysis of YYYYMMDD palindromes counts 366 in total across history, with none at all between 31 August 1380 and 2 October 2001 — a gap of more than six centuries in which the format produced nothing.

Rarity, though, is format-relative. 29 February 2092 is unremarkable in US order (02292092 reversed is 29029220) and in ISO order (20920229 reversed is 92202902). A date is only “the rarest” once the format is named — which returns to the rule from section 2: always name the format, or the claim means nothing.

8. Limits of this calculator

The supported range is years 1 to 9999, in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. The tool does not convert BC dates: year numbering before year 1 (there is no year zero in the BC/AD system) is a separate source of errors, and the tool declines to guess rather than risk a wrong verdict. Dates from before the Gregorian reform are labelled proleptically, so a historical date recorded in the Julian calendar may carry a different label here — the tool checks the label you enter, not the historical day behind it.

The tool computes zero-padded formats only. The unpadded palindrome weeks of section 6 are explained but not computed: to check an unpadded date by hand, write the month without a leading zero, the day with two digits and the last two digits of the year, then reverse the string. And the tool checks digit strings only — it does not judge whether a palindrome date is lucky, significant or worth celebrating. That judgement belongs to the person reading the result, not to the arithmetic.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a palindrome date?

A date whose digits read the same forwards and backwards once the separators are removed and the day and month are zero-padded. 02/02/2020 reads 02022020 in US and European 8-digit form and 20200202 in ISO form — a palindrome in all three.

Why is 2 February 2020 special?

It is a palindrome in all three 8-digit formats (US, European and ISO), which is rare: most palindrome dates work in only one format.

Why does the format change the answer?

Because each format orders the digits differently. 2 March 2030 is a palindrome in US form (03022030) and ISO form (20300302) but not in European form (02032030).

What is the next palindrome date?

Enter a date and the tool shows the next palindrome date after it. From 3 November 2026, the next US-format palindrome date is 2 March 2030, and the next European-format one is 3 February 2030.

How many palindrome dates are there in the 21st century?

12 in US 8-digit form (first 2 October 2001, last 2 September 2090) and 29 in European 8-digit form (first 10 February 2001, last 29 February 2092).

What are palindrome weeks?

In unpadded month-day-year form, most years since 2011 have had 10 consecutive palindrome days — 2020 has none, and 2022 has 9 (20–28 February). In July 2027 they run from 20 to 29 July (72027 to 72927).

Can I check my birthday?

Yes — enter it and pick a format. The tool also shows the previous and next palindrome dates in that format, so you can see how close your birthday is to one.

Does it work for old dates?

Yes, years 1 to 9999 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. 11 November 1111 reads 11111111 in all 8-digit forms. Dates before the Gregorian reform are labelled proleptically, not as they were written at the time.

Why are there no palindrome dates in some years?

Because a palindrome needs the year’s reverse to be a valid month and day. In US 8-digit form the date must fall on the 2nd of a month, and the month must be the reverse of the year’s last two digits — most years fail one of those tests.

What is the last palindrome date of the century?

In US 8-digit form, 2 September 2090. In European form, the leap day 29 February 2092.

What is the rarest palindrome date?

The leap-day palindrome 29 February 2092 in European form: it is the only 29 February palindrome of the century, and the last European-format palindrome of the century.

11. Methodology & Data Integrity

Method: the engine converts each calendar date to an integer day count (days from the civil date, proleptic Gregorian, years 1–9999) and back, using integer arithmetic only — no Date objects, so years 1–99 convert correctly. The digit string for a format is built by zero-padding the day and month to two digits and the year to four digits (8-digit formats) or two digits (6-digit formats), joined in the format’s order with no separators. A date is a palindrome in a format when that string equals its own reversal. Previous, next and next-10 results come from stepping the day count one day at a time and testing each day; the weekday comes from the same day count.

Verification: the engine’s full-century lists were checked against published palindrome counts — 12 US-format and 29 European-format palindrome dates in the 21st century, with the US list matching the published first (2 October 2001) and last (2 September 2090) dates and the European list matching its published first (10 February 2001) and last (29 February 2092) dates. The millennium US-format count (36 dates, 2001–3000, last 22 September 2290) also matches the published analysis. The structural patterns were asserted on the full lists, not sampled: every US-format palindrome date of the century falls on the 2nd of a month, every European-format one falls in February, and 29 February 2092 is the only leap-day palindrome of the century in any 8-digit format. Famous dates were checked individually: 2 February 2020 in all three 8-digit formats, 2 March 2030 in US and ISO but not European form, and 11 November 1111 in all three 8-digit forms.

Worked examples in this page were computed by the same engine: 2 February 2020 (02022020 / 02022020 / 20200202); 2 March 2030 (03022030 / 20300302, not 02032030); 2 December 2021 (12022021); 2 October 2001 (10022001); 29 February 2092 (29022092); July 2027 unpadded week (72027–72927).

Limits: Gregorian calendar only, proleptic before 1582; years 1–9999 only, no BC dates; zero-padded formats computed by the tool, unpadded weeks explained but not computed; digit strings only, no claims about luck or significance.
