Working Out School Starting Age

School entry compares a child's date of birth with an official cutoff date, and the cutoff is local, specific and subject to change.

Updated August 16, 2026·About 3 minutes·745 words·Original educational guide
Written and maintained byNavjeet KambojAge Calculator Lab author & editor

Quick answer

Calculate exact age on the cutoff date, then apply the current published rule for the specific authority. Both halves matter.

Two things, calculated separately

The arithmetic is: how old is this child on the cutoff date. The policy is: what does the authority do with that answer. Keeping them apart is what makes the result checkable, because the arithmetic is stable and the policy is not.

Collapsing them — deciding a child 'is a September starter' without writing down the age and the rule — produces a conclusion nobody can verify, including you in twelve months.

Cutoffs vary more than people expect

England uses 31 August, so the school year runs from September to August. Scotland uses late February with a deferral right. Many US states use 1 September but a substantial number use other dates, and some leave it to individual districts. Australia varies by state, with several offering a window rather than a fixed date.

The practical consequence is that a rule from a neighbouring area, an older sibling's experience, or a general article is not evidence. Only the current published policy of the responsible authority is.

The summer-born question

In systems with a fixed cutoff, children born just before it start school as the youngest in their cohort — nearly a year younger than the oldest. There is a substantial body of research on relative age effects, and several systems now offer deferral or delayed-entry routes in response.

Whether such a route exists, who decides, and what evidence is needed varies entirely by authority. If this is your situation, the question to ask is procedural — what is the process and what is the deadline — rather than whether the child is 'ready', which is a separate conversation.

Boundary cases

A birth date within a few days of a cutoff is a signal to verify rather than conclude. Check the date of birth against the birth certificate, check the cutoff against the current published policy, and check whether the wording is 'on or before' or 'before'.

That last one is not pedantry: a child born on the cutoff date itself qualifies under one reading and not the other, and both wordings appear in real policy documents.

Worked example

Under a 31 August cutoff, a child born 31 August 2021 is 4 years 0 days on 31 August 2025 and starts that September. A child born 1 September 2021 is 3 years 364 days and waits a full year. One day of birth separates a whole school year.

None of this is difficult arithmetic. The part worth slowing down for is deciding which convention applies before you start.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using today's age instead of age on the cutoff. The cutoff date is the reference date. Today's age is irrelevant to the rule.
  • Assuming a neighbouring authority's cutoff. Cutoffs are local and change. Check the current published policy for the specific authority.
  • Rounding age up near the boundary. Rules use exact dates. Rounding is how a child ends up in the wrong cohort on paper.

Frequently asked questions

Can a child start early or late?

Some systems allow deferral or delayed entry with a process attached. Whether one exists, and who decides, is entirely authority-specific.

Does the calculator determine eligibility?

No. It gives the exact age on the date you specify. The authority's current rule determines what that age means.

What if two sources give different cutoffs?

Use the responsible authority's own current publication. Secondary sources go out of date and are frequently wrong.
