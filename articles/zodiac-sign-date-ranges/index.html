Western Zodiac Date Ranges and Why Sources Disagree

Sun-sign dates come from a fixed tropical scheme, but cusp dates shift by a day between years and between sources.

Quick answer

The commonly published ranges are approximations of an event that moves slightly each year. Near a boundary, sources will disagree — and none of them is measuring anything.

Where the dates come from

Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, which divides the year into twelve 30-degree segments starting at the March equinox. The equinox does not fall at the same instant every year — it drifts across roughly a day and a half and resets on the leap-year cycle.

That means every sign boundary moves slightly year to year. Published tables give a representative date, which is why one source says Aries begins on 20 March and another says the 21st. Both are simplifications of something that genuinely varies.

The cusp question

"Born on the cusp" is a popular idea with no formal standing. A birth date either falls before a boundary or after it, and in a chart calculated properly there is no ambiguity — only a specific degree.

Practitioners who calculate charts precisely use the birth time and place to resolve boundary cases, since the transition happens at a particular moment rather than at midnight. General-purpose lookups using date alone cannot do that, which is exactly why they disagree at the edges.

Tropical and sidereal

The tropical zodiac is fixed to the seasons; the sidereal zodiac used in Vedic astrology is fixed to the constellations. Because of precession, the two have drifted about 24 degrees apart, which is most of a sign.

So the same birth date gives different signs in the two systems, and that is not a disagreement about the date — it is two different schemes. Anyone comparing a Western reading with a Vedic one is comparing two systems, not checking one against the other.

What this is

A cultural tradition with a long history, widely used for entertainment, self-reflection and conversation. It has no predictive validity that has survived controlled testing, and the constellations the signs are named for no longer align with the dates.

The calculator here treats it as what it is: a lookup against a conventional date table, labelled as a cultural reference rather than a measurement. It is included because people want it, not because it measures anything.

Worked example

A birth on 20 March falls in Pisces or Aries depending on the year and the source, because the equinox moves. A birth on 5 August is Leo in every Western table and Cancer in the sidereal system used in Vedic astrology — the same date read under two different schemes.

The twelve ranges, side by side

It helps to see the conventional table whole, because most disagreements involve exactly one cell of it. The commonly published tropical ranges are: Capricorn, 22 December to 19 January; Aquarius, 20 January to 18 February; Pisces, 19 February to 20 March; Aries, 21 March to 19 April; Taurus, 20 April to 20 May; Gemini, 21 May to 20 June; Cancer, 21 June to 22 July; Leo, 23 July to 22 August; Virgo, 23 August to 22 September; Libra, 23 September to 22 October; Scorpio, 23 October to 21 November; and Sagittarius, 22 November to 21 December. Every published table you are likely to meet is this list, with one or two boundary dates nudged by a day.

Counted in days, the ranges are not the equal twelfths of the year the tidy table suggests. Capricorn spans 29 days; Aries, Pisces, Aquarius, Libra, Scorpio and Sagittarius span 30; Taurus, Gemini, Leo and Virgo span 31; and Cancer spans 32. The twelve spans add back to exactly 365, so nothing is lost — the inequality comes from the calendar underneath, whose months run 28 to 31 days while the signs are defined by 30-degree segments of the Sun's path. A sign is a segment of a circle, not a block of the calendar, and the date ranges are only ever a translation of the first into the second. Translations of that kind are where the one-day wobbles live.

The boundary walk shows how small the contested territory really is. Take three March birthdays and read them against the two Aries conventions in circulation, one starting Aries on 20 March and the other on 21 March. A birth on 19 March is Pisces under both. A birth on 21 March is Aries under both. Only the middle date, 20 March, changes its answer with the source. The pattern repeats at all twelve boundaries: the disagreement is confined to the boundary date itself, and every date a day or more inside a range is stable across sources. Out of 365 possible birthdays, the twelve boundary dates are the only ones a general table can get 'wrong' — and on those dates, as the cusp discussion above explains, only a chart calculated from the birth time and place settles the question at all.

So the working rule is narrow. If your birthday falls anywhere except on one of the twelve boundary dates, every Western table will give you the same sign and the choice of source is irrelevant. If it does fall on a boundary date, the honest answer is not a sign but a sentence: the date sits on the boundary, tables place the changeover on that date or the one beside it depending on the year, and the sign is whichever side of the exact transition moment the birth fell. That is less satisfying than a single word, but it is the only version of the answer that is actually true.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming one published table is authoritative. Boundaries move slightly each year. Sources differ at the edges and none is definitive.
  • Treating 'cusp' as a category. It has no formal standing. A properly calculated chart places the birth on one side or the other.
  • Comparing a tropical sign with a sidereal one. They are different systems, drifted about 24 degrees apart. Disagreement is expected.

Frequently asked questions

Why do sources give different dates?

Sign boundaries follow the equinox, which moves by up to a day and a half across the leap-year cycle. Published tables round it.

What if I was born on a boundary date?

A chart calculated with your birth time and place resolves it exactly. A date-only lookup cannot.

Do the signs match the constellations?

No. Precession has moved them apart over roughly two millennia, and the constellations also vary in size.
