Calculating Age at Marriage for Records and Genealogy

Age on a wedding date is a straightforward calendar calculation, but the records it comes from often are not.

Quick answer

Calculate the exact age on the ceremony date, and where a source gives only a year or a stated age, record a range rather than inventing precision.

The calculation is the easy part

Age at marriage is exact age on the ceremony date: complete years, then complete months, then remaining days from the date of birth. There is no special convention and no ambiguity, provided both dates are known precisely.

The reason it is worth a dedicated calculation is that the reference date is almost always misremembered as today. Anyone reconstructing a record years later needs the age on the ceremony date, not the age of the person now — and defaulting to today is the single most common error in this kind of work.

Historical records rarely give you both dates

Marriage registers frequently record a stated age rather than a date of birth, and stated ages were often rounded, estimated or deliberately adjusted — to meet a minimum age, to reduce an apparent gap, or simply because the person did not know. "Of full age" appears in place of a number in many older English registers and means only that the person was over twenty-one.

When a record gives an age and a date, the implied birth window is roughly a year wide, because the birthday may or may not have passed. Record it as a range. Converting a stated age into a single birth date creates false precision that later researchers will inherit as fact.

Two calendars, one date

Records from before the Gregorian changeover need care. Britain and its colonies adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1752, dropping eleven days and moving the start of the year from 25 March to 1 January. Other countries changed at different times, from 1582 onwards.

This produces the double-dating convention seen in older documents — 1 February 1718/19, for example. When calculating an interval across such a boundary, note which calendar each date belongs to. Modern date arithmetic assumes a proleptic Gregorian calendar and will not account for the missing days.

Anniversary milestones from the same date

Once the marriage date is established, milestone anniversaries follow the same calendar-year addition as birthdays: add the milestone in whole years to the start date. A twenty-fifth anniversary is the date twenty-five complete years after the ceremony, not 9,131 days later.

The same leap-day consideration applies. A 29 February wedding date needs a stated convention for non-leap years, and for a family record the choice matters less than writing it down.

Worked example

A person born 18 November 1994 marrying on 12 June 2026 was 31 years, 6 months and 25 days old. If a register instead recorded only "aged 31" on that date, the implied birth window runs from 13 June 1994 to 12 June 1995 — a full year, not a single date.

Work through it with your own dates and the difference will usually be obvious. If it is not, the convention in use is the first thing to check.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving today as the reference date. The ceremony date controls the calculation. This is the most common error in record reconstruction.
  • Converting a stated age to a single birth date. A stated age implies a year-wide window. Record the range.
  • Ignoring calendar changeovers in old records. Pre-1752 British dates may use a different year start and calendar. Note which applies before calculating intervals.

Frequently asked questions

What does 'of full age' mean in an old register?

That the person was above the age of majority, historically twenty-one. It is not a specific age and should not be treated as one.

Should I use the licence date or the ceremony date?

The ceremony date, unless you are specifically recording age at licensing. Note which you used either way.

How do I handle a stated age that conflicts with a known birth date?

Record both and note the discrepancy. Stated ages in registers were often approximate or adjusted.
