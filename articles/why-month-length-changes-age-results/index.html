Why Month Length Changes the Answer

Months run from 28 to 31 days, so any calculation that treats them as a fixed size drifts away from the calendar immediately.

Quick answer

Preserve calendar boundaries when the output is years, months and days. Use total days only when a single-unit duration is what was asked for.

The 30-day assumption and where it fails

Treating a month as 30 days is convenient and wrong in eleven months out of twelve. Seven months have 31 days, four have 30, and February has 28 or 29. The assumption is exactly right in April, June, September and November and nowhere else.

Over a single month the error is small. Over a year of month-by-month arithmetic it accumulates to about five days, which is why a 30-day-month model and a calendar model give visibly different answers for anything measured in months over a long span.

Borrowing, and why it goes wrong

Manual age calculation usually works column by column: subtract days, subtract months, subtract years, borrowing when a column goes negative. The borrow step is where the month length enters, and where people default to thirty.

The correct borrow takes the actual length of the month preceding the reference date. Borrowing from a February gives 28 or 29; from a March, 31. Using thirty in either case shifts the days field by up to three, which is enough to cross an assessment band or a policy boundary.

End-of-month arithmetic

Adding a month to 31 January has no exact answer, because February has no 31st. The two standard responses are to clamp to the last day of the target month, or to roll forward into the next. Both appear in real systems, and they differ by up to three days.

This matters most for anything recurring monthly from a date near the end of a month — subscriptions, medication schedules, contractual milestones. Whichever convention applies, the important thing is that it does not change between calculations, because a sequence built on two conventions cannot be reconstructed.

When to abandon months entirely

If what you actually need is a duration rather than a calendar structure, days are cleaner. Total elapsed days is unambiguous, needs no convention, and handles leap years automatically because every day in the span is simply counted.

Use months when the question is calendar-shaped — anniversaries, monthly cycles, age in a form that asks for months. Use days when the question is about quantity. Problems arise when a days answer is reported as months, or the reverse, without saying so.

Worked example

From 31 January to 28 February is 28 days, and by most conventions exactly one calendar month. From 31 March to 30 April is 30 days, and also one calendar month. Two intervals of different lengths described identically — which is correct, and which is why a month is not a unit of duration.

The shape of the calculation stays the same with your own figures. What changes is the source of the rule, so check that before treating the number as settled.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a month is 30 days. It is only true four months of the year. The error accumulates to about five days annually.
  • Borrowing a flat thirty in the days column. Borrow the real length of the preceding month, or the days field is off by up to three.
  • Switching end-of-month conventions between calculations. Clamping and rolling both exist. Inconsistency makes a sequence irreproducible.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average month length?

About 30.44 days, but that average is not useful for calendar arithmetic — it belongs to statistical work, not to counting anniversaries.

Should I use months or days?

Months for calendar-shaped questions, days for durations. State which you used, since the two answers differ.

Which end-of-month rule does this site use?

Clamping to the last day of the target month, which is the more common convention. Where a governing rule specifies otherwise, that rule wins.
