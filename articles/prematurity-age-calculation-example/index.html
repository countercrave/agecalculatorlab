Corrected Age: A Worked Example, Step by Step

Doing the two calculations separately — chronological age, then the prematurity interval — is what keeps the result reproducible.

Updated August 16, 2026·About 4 minutes·809 words·Original educational guide

Quick answer

Work in days: calculate chronological age in days, subtract weeks early converted to days, then express the result. Converting to months early introduces error.

Step one: weeks early

Weeks early is 40 minus the gestational age at birth. A baby born at 32 weeks is 8 weeks early; at 34 weeks, 6 weeks early; at 28 weeks, 12 weeks early. Use the documented gestational age from the birth record rather than a remembered figure, and note if it was itself an estimate.

Where gestational age is recorded with days as well — 32 weeks and 4 days, for instance — include them. The interval is 40 weeks minus 32 weeks 4 days, which is 7 weeks 3 days, not 8 weeks. Rounding here carries straight through to the final answer.

Step two: chronological age in days

Calculate the elapsed days from the birth date to the date you care about. Days rather than months, because the subtraction in the next step is in days and mixing units is where the error enters.

This is ordinary date arithmetic, and it handles leap days automatically because every day in the span is simply counted. A baby born 12 January 2026 and measured on 16 August 2026 has lived 216 days.

Step three: subtract, then convert

Subtract the prematurity interval in days from the chronological age in days. Eight weeks is 56 days, so 216 minus 56 is 160 days. Only now convert to whatever unit you want to present: 160 days is 22 weeks and 6 days, or roughly five months.

Doing the conversion last is the whole point. Subtracting "two months" from "seven months" gives five months, but two months is 59 to 62 days rather than 56, so that route can be several days out — enough to matter when the figure is being read at week resolution.

Presenting both figures

The output worth recording is chronological age, corrected age, and the gestational age at birth that connects them. With those three, anyone can verify the arithmetic and see immediately why the two ages differ.

If the corrected age would be negative — which happens when a very preterm baby is still young — that means the baby has not yet reached their original due date. It is not an error, and the honest presentation is to say so rather than to clamp the number to zero without explanation.

Worked example

Born 12 January 2026 at 32 weeks; measured 16 August 2026. Chronological age: 216 days (7 months 4 days). Weeks early: 40 − 32 = 8 weeks = 56 days. Corrected: 216 − 56 = 160 days = 22 weeks 6 days, about 5 months. Subtracting "2 months" from "7 months" would have given a figure up to six days different.

Try it with the dates in front of you. Where the answer sits close to a threshold, treat that as a prompt to check the rule rather than to conclude.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Rounding gestational age to whole weeks. 32 weeks 4 days gives a 7-week-3-day interval, not 8 weeks. The rounding carries into the final answer.
  • Subtracting months from months. Two calendar months is 59 to 62 days, not the 56 days that eight weeks represents. Work in days and convert last.
  • Hiding a negative corrected age. It means the baby has not reached their original due date. Say so rather than clamping to zero silently.

Frequently asked questions

Why work in days rather than months?

Because the prematurity interval is measured in weeks. Mixing weeks and calendar months introduces a several-day error.

What if gestational age at birth is uncertain?

Use the documented figure and note the uncertainty. The corrected age inherits it, and a reader should be able to see that.

Can corrected age be negative?

Yes, for a very preterm baby who has not yet reached their original due date. It is a meaningful result, not an error.
