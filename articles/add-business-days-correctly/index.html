Adding Business Days Without Losing Count

Adding business days means stepping forward one day at a time and only counting the ones that qualify — not multiplying by a ratio.

Quick answer

Step and count. Approximating with a five-sevenths ratio drifts as soon as a holiday appears or the start day falls at a weekend.

Why the ratio approach fails

The tempting shortcut is to multiply business days by seven fifths and add that many calendar days. Ten business days becomes fourteen calendar days. It is right often enough to feel reliable and wrong whenever the start day or a holiday breaks the pattern.

Starting on a Friday, ten business days lands on the Friday two weeks later — fourteen calendar days, which the ratio gets right. Starting on a Saturday, it lands sixteen calendar days out, because the first two days count for nothing. The shortcut has no way to know that.

The correct procedure

Move forward one calendar day at a time from the start date. For each day, check whether it is a weekend day under the applicable pattern and whether it is a public holiday in the applicable calendar. If it is neither, decrement the counter. Stop when the counter reaches zero.

The date you stop on is the answer, and it is by construction a working day. That last property is worth noting: a business-day shift can never land on a weekend or a holiday, unlike a calendar-day shift.

The start day does not count

The standard convention is that counting begins on the first working day after the start date, so 'one business day from Monday' is Tuesday. Some documents intend the start day to count, making it Monday.

This is the same inclusive-exclusive question that affects every date count, and it is worth resolving before a deadline rather than after. The difference is exactly one working day, which can be three calendar days over a weekend.

Backwards is not symmetric

Counting backwards works the same way in reverse, but the result is not the mirror image. Add ten business days to a date and subtract ten from the result and you will usually return to the start — but not if the original date was itself a weekend or holiday, because the forward step skipped it and the backward step lands on the nearest working day instead.

For scheduling backwards from a deadline, count backwards directly rather than computing forwards and inverting. It is the same amount of work and it gives the answer to the question you actually asked.

Worked example

Twenty working days from Tuesday 1 December 2026 lands on 29 December with no holiday calendar, and on 31 December under an England & Wales calendar — because Christmas Day and the Boxing Day substitute both fall inside the span. The ratio shortcut predicts 28 December and misses both.

The example is there to show the shape of the working. Your own dates will behave the same way; the rule that governs them may not.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Multiplying by seven fifths. It ignores where the start day falls and every holiday in the span.
  • Assuming the start day counts. The usual convention starts the count on the next working day. Check the document.
  • Inverting a forward calculation to go backwards. The two are not symmetric when the anchor date is not itself a working day. Count backwards directly.

Frequently asked questions

Does the result ever land on a weekend?

No. By construction it lands on a working day, which is one practical difference from a calendar-day shift.

Does the calculator apply public holidays?

Yes, for twelve jurisdictions with substitute days. It reports how many holidays it skipped.

What about half days and company shutdowns?

Not covered. Adjust the result manually, since those vary by employer rather than by jurisdiction.
