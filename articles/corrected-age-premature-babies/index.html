Corrected Age for Premature Babies: A Parent's Guide

Corrected age subtracts how early a baby arrived from their chronological age, so development is compared against the right timeline.

Quick answer

Corrected age = chronological age minus weeks born early. Use it for milestone conversations, keep chronological age for records, and let your baby's clinical team decide which applies when.

Why two ages exist

A baby born at 32 weeks has been outside the womb for the same number of days as any other baby born on that date, but they have had eight fewer weeks of development. Comparing them against milestone charts built for babies born at term measures the wrong thing, and it consistently makes preterm babies look behind when they are on track for their own timeline.

Corrected age fixes the comparison by subtracting the prematurity interval. A six-month-old born eight weeks early has a corrected age of about four months, and four months is the fair reference point for what they are doing now.

How the subtraction works

Weeks early is 40 minus the gestational age at birth, using the documented figure from the birth record rather than an estimate. A baby born at 32 weeks is 8 weeks early; at 28 weeks, 12 weeks early.

That interval is then subtracted from chronological age. The important detail is that it is subtracted as weeks, not converted into months first. Eight weeks is 56 days; two calendar months is 59 to 62. Converting early introduces an error of several days into a figure that is often being read at week-level resolution.

Where each age belongs

Corrected age is the one to use when thinking about development: rolling, sitting, babbling, feeding skills, and the milestone charts in a red book or equivalent. It is also the age most clinical teams will use in developmental conversations during the first couple of years.

Chronological age stays for everything official and everything social. It is the age on the birth certificate, the age for immunisation schedules in most protocols, the age for nursery and school entry, and the age at the birthday party. Neither age replaces the other, and a record that shows both is clearer than one that picks.

What this is and is not

The calculation is arithmetic and it is exact. What it cannot do is tell you whether your baby is developing typically. Corrected age puts the comparison on the right footing; interpreting what you see against it is the job of your paediatric or neonatal team, who know your baby's history.

If something is worrying you — a milestone that feels late even against corrected age, a change in feeding, anything that has shifted — that is a conversation to have with them rather than a calculation to redo. Bringing both ages and the gestational age at birth to the appointment makes that conversation faster.

Worked example

A baby born on 12 January 2026 at 32 weeks gestation, seen on 16 August 2026, is 7 months and 4 days old chronologically — 216 days. They were 8 weeks early, which is 56 days. Corrected age is 160 days, or about 22 weeks and 6 days: roughly five months rather than seven.

Numbers from a worked example are illustrative, not precedent. Run yours, keep the inputs beside the result, and confirm any rule that carries a consequence.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Converting weeks early into months before subtracting. Subtract the interval in days or weeks. Converting first adds a several-day error to a figure often read at week resolution.
  • Using an estimated gestational age without noting it. Take the figure from the birth record. If it is an estimate, write that down alongside it.
  • Treating corrected age as a diagnosis or a reassurance. It sets up a fair comparison. What the comparison means is a clinical judgement, not a calculator output.

Frequently asked questions

Which age do I give at appointments?

Bring both, plus the gestational age at birth. Clinical teams generally want chronological age for records and corrected age for development.

Does corrected age apply to immunisations?

Immunisation schedules usually run on chronological age, but follow the schedule your clinical team gives you rather than a general rule.

Is corrected age used for birthdays?

No. Birthdays follow the actual birth date. Corrected age is a developmental reference, not an alternative date of birth.
