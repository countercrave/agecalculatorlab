Estimating a Tree's Age From Its Trunk

Measure the circumference, convert to diameter, multiply by a species growth factor — and treat the result as a broad estimate.

Quick answer

The method gives a usable range, not a planting year. Site conditions can change growth rate by a factor of two or more.

The measurement

Measure the circumference at breast height — conventionally 1.3 to 1.4 metres above ground, on the uphill side if the ground slopes. Divide by π to get the diameter. Multiply the diameter in inches by the species growth factor to get an estimated age in years.

The single most common error is using circumference where the formula wants diameter, which overstates age by more than three times. If your estimate says a garden tree is 400 years old, check that first.

Growth factors are species-specific

Fast-growing species like poplar and silver maple have low factors around 2 to 3; slow-growing species like oak and beech run around 4 to 5. Using the wrong species factor changes the answer by a similar proportion.

That makes correct identification part of the calculation rather than a preliminary. If you are unsure of the species, the honest output is a range spanning the plausible factors rather than a single figure.

Why the estimate is broad

Growth rate depends on light, water, soil, competition, damage, disease and management. An open-grown parkland oak puts on girth far faster than the same species crowded in woodland, and the difference over a century is enormous.

Urban trees are affected further by compacted soil, restricted root volume, pollution and pruning. A street tree and a field tree of the same species and girth can differ in age by decades.

When precision actually matters

For a tree preservation order, a planning application, a boundary dispute or heritage designation, an estimate is not evidence. Increment coring gives a much better answer by counting actual rings, and an arboriculturist can do it without significant harm to a healthy tree.

For curiosity, teaching or a rough sense of a tree's history, the girth method is entirely adequate — provided the answer is presented as an approximation, which is what it is.

Worked example

An oak with a circumference of 250 cm has a diameter of about 80 cm, or 31 inches. At a growth factor of 4 that gives roughly 126 years. Using circumference instead of diameter would give about 394 years — an error of more than three times, from one skipped division.

Work through it with your own dates and the difference will usually be obvious. If it is not, the convention in use is the first thing to check.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using circumference instead of diameter. Divide by π first. Skipping it overstates age by more than three times.
  • Guessing the species factor. Factors range from about 2 to 5. The wrong one is wrong by a similar proportion.
  • Presenting the estimate as a planting year. Site conditions can change growth rate twofold. Give a range.

Frequently asked questions

What height should I measure at?

Breast height, conventionally 1.3 to 1.4 metres, on the uphill side where the ground slopes. Measuring lower inflates girth and age.

How accurate is the method?

Broadly indicative. Plus or minus a quarter is optimistic for a tree of unknown history.

Can I count rings without felling?

Yes — an increment borer extracts a narrow core. An arboriculturist can do it with minimal impact on a healthy tree.
